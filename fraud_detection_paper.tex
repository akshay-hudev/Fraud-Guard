\abstract{\mathversion{normal}\textbf{Purpose:} Graph neural networks (GNNs) are
proposed for healthcare insurance fraud because collusive provider rings
leave traces in claim networks, but when they beat tabular
models is unclear.
\textbf{Methods:} We built a synthetic benchmark that independently
varies the feature-level signal and the doctor--patient density of fraud
rings, tests on rings of unseen doctors and patients, and analyses audit
periods separately to avoid leakage. We compared 14 equally tuned methods,
including gradient boosting with and without engineered graph features and
five heterogeneous GNNs, over 54 worlds in nine regimes,
evaluated routing from gradient boosting
to a GNN, and checked the findings against public provider-labelled
Medicare-style data (5{,}410 providers).
\textbf{Results:} Without a feature-level signal, claim-only models were at
chance, while per-entity aggregates detected most rings (average precision
0.89--0.93); structure added up to 0.10, only for moderately or densely
interlinked rings. Pooled over worlds, no GNN
outperformed gradient boosting with engineered graph features (GraphSAGE
$-0.0001$, HGT $-0.011$), and GNNs were 0.08--0.15 worse on isolated fraud.
Routing high-risk claims to
a GNN gained nothing; fusing both models' rankings gained at most 0.006. Test
rings reusing training-ring members inflated performance by up to 0.60. On real data, gradient boosting on provider aggregates was best
(0.704); graph information did not help.
\textbf{Conclusion:} Relational modelling pays off only for dense collusion
and then mainly through structure that simple engineered features
capture. Graph models should be evaluated against graph-augmented tabular
baselines on unseen entities.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Health insurance fraud causes losses estimated in the tens of billions of
dollars each year in the United States alone~\cite{nhcaa}. It takes two
broad forms. In \emph{isolated} fraud a single provider inflates or
fabricates individual claims, so the evidence lies in the claim itself:
amounts, procedure counts or coding that are unusual for the provider's
specialty. In \emph{collusive} fraud a group of providers and recruited
patients file claims together. Each claim can look ordinary; the evidence
lies in who bills whom---patients who see many members of the same small
group of providers, and providers who share an unusually large fraction of
their patients.

The second form motivates graph neural networks (GNNs) over the
patient--provider--facility network, in particular heterogeneous
architectures such as RGCN~\cite{schlichtkrull2018}, HAN~\cite{wang2019han},
HGT~\cite{hu2020hgt} and Simple-HGN~\cite{lv2021}. Whether GNNs are needed,
however, is an empirical question. Gradient-boosted trees remain strong on
tabular data~\cite{grinsztajn2022}, relational signals can be engineered as
features, and benchmark studies outside healthcare report that tree
ensembles with neighbourhood features often match or beat GNNs on graph
anomaly detection~\cite{weber2019elliptic,tang2023gadbench}.

Answering the question for healthcare claims is difficult for two reasons.
First, public claims data with labelled collusion are not available, and
real labels conflate fraud typologies. Second, evaluation is fragile:
identifiers that encode the label, statistics computed on the full dataset,
and providers that appear in both training and test data inflate apparent
performance~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}. Fraud rings make the
last problem acute, because a model can score a test claim correctly by
recognising a provider it saw labelled during training instead of
recognising coordination.

We therefore study the question in a controlled setting and check it on
real data. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A regime-controllable benchmark.} A synthetic claims generator
  in which the feature-level signal of ring claims ($\delta$) and the density
  of the ring's doctor--patient block ($s$) are varied independently, with an
  \emph{emergent} protocol in which every test ring is formed by doctors and
  patients never seen in a training ring, and an audit-period design that
  keeps features and graphs free of future information
  (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}). Code, generator and tests are released.
\item \textbf{A controlled comparison} of 14 methods---tabular models with and
  without engineered graph features, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) control,
  five heterogeneous GNNs,
  two hybrids and an unsupervised overlap score---with an equal tuning
  budget, nine regimes, six independently generated worlds per regime and
  paired statistics, together with an evaluation of two-stage routing from
  gradient boosting to a GNN (Sections~\ref{sec:methods} and~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{Mechanism and memorisation analyses}: degree-preserving
  rewiring, single-factor ablations of HGT (depth, attention heads,
  relations, node inputs), an analysis of which inputs the trained models
  rely on, and a comparison of new versus recurring rings that quantifies
  how much apparent ring detection comes from recognising known members
  (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). A re-analysis of a small fixed benchmark
  shows how such data can suggest the opposite conclusion
  (Section~\ref{sec:legacy}).
\item \textbf{A real-data check} on a public, provider-labelled
  Medicare-style claims dataset with a provider-disjoint protocol
  (Section~\ref{sec:realdata}). Its labels identify providers, not rings,
  so it tests whether graph information helps provider-level detection,
  not whether rings are detected.
\end{enumerate}

Our main findings are as follows. Claim features alone cannot reveal rings
whose claims look legitimate, but per-entity aggregates capture most of the
evidence, because ring members are unusually active. Coordination structure
adds measurable value only when the ring's doctor--patient block is dense.
Where it does, gradient boosting with a few engineered shared-patient features
is as good as or better than every GNN we tested; GNNs that also receive those
features gain a small but consistent amount ($+0.008$ in average precision,
AP, pooled over all worlds). GNNs are
clearly worse on isolated fraud, because message passing dilutes claim-level
anomalies. HGT's ring detection is structural: it collapses without degree
encodings and survives with degree encodings as its only inputs. Routing
high-risk claims from gradient boosting to a GNN gained nothing; fusing the
two models' rankings gained at most 0.006 AP, as an ensemble rather than as a
router. Evaluations that let test rings share members with training
rings overstate ring detection, by up to 0.60 AP for a model that only sees
entity attributes. On the real data, where labels show no relational
assortativity, no graph-based method improved on gradient boosting over
provider aggregates.


\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Tabular healthcare fraud detection.}
Most published healthcare-fraud detectors are supervised classifiers over
claim- or provider-level features. On Medicare data labelled with the
federal exclusion list (LEIE), Bauder and Khoshgoftaar~\cite{bauder2017}
compared classical learners, Herland et al.~\cite{herland2018} combined
several Medicare data sources, and Johnson and
Khoshgoftaar~\cite{johnson2019} studied neural networks under extreme class
imbalance. Earlier work on Medicaid data used multidimensional data models
and outlier analysis~\cite{thornton2013}. These models score each claim or
provider from its own attributes and pre-computed aggregates; relational
information enters, if at all, through hand-engineered features.

\paragraph{Graph-based fraud and anomaly detection.}
Surveys of graph-based anomaly detection~\cite{akoglu2015,pourhabibi2020}
document how coordination leaves structural traces such as dense blocks,
lockstep behaviour and unusual egonets. Unsupervised detectors exploit these
traces directly, e.g.\ OddBall~\cite{akoglu2010oddball},
CopyCatch~\cite{beutel2013copycatch} and FRAUDAR~\cite{hooi2016fraudar}.
Graph neural networks (GNNs) have been applied to review
fraud~\cite{wang2019fdgars}, underground-forum key players~\cite{zhang2019player2vec},
and camouflaged or class-imbalanced fraudsters~\cite{dou2020care,liu2021pcgnn};
spectral analyses argue that anomalies shift graph spectra in ways that
standard message passing can blur~\cite{tang2022bwgnn}.

\paragraph{Heterogeneous GNNs.}
Relational GCN~\cite{schlichtkrull2018} uses relation-specific weights; HAN~\cite{wang2019han}
aggregates over hand-chosen meta-paths with node- and semantic-level
attention; HGT~\cite{hu2020hgt} parameterises attention by node and edge
type without meta-paths; and Simple-HGN~\cite{lv2021} showed that a GAT
with edge-type embeddings, residual attention and output normalisation is
competitive with more elaborate heterogeneous architectures when baselines
are tuned fairly.

\paragraph{When do graph models beat tabular models?}
Evidence on this question is mixed. On the Elliptic Bitcoin dataset a random
forest on node features outperformed graph convolutional networks under a
temporal split~\cite{weber2019elliptic}. GADBench~\cite{tang2023gadbench}
found that tree ensembles with simple neighbourhood aggregation are among the
strongest supervised graph anomaly detectors across ten datasets, and tree
ensembles remain hard to beat on tabular data generally~\cite{grinsztajn2022}.
Synthetic benchmarks with injected typologies make the question
testable: Altman et al.~\cite{altman2023aml} generate money-laundering
patterns with known ground truth, and Jesus et al.~\cite{jesus2022baf}
release controlled tabular fraud suites. To our knowledge no comparable
controlled study exists for heterogeneous patient--provider--facility claim
graphs, where the relevant typology is the collusive provider ring.

\paragraph{Evaluation pitfalls.}
Leakage through features that encode the target, through preprocessing fitted
on the full dataset, or through entities shared between training and test
data inflates reported performance~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}.
GNN benchmarks are additionally sensitive to split choice and tuning
effort~\cite{shchur2018pitfalls}. These risks are acute for fraud rings,
whose members recur across claims: a model can score a test claim correctly
by recognising a provider it saw labelled in training rather than by
recognising coordination.



\section{Benchmark Design}\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Synthetic claims world}\label{sec:generator}
We simulate $6{,}000$ patients, $300$ doctors and $40$ hospitals in five
regions over four audit periods of six months (Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}; Table~\ref{tab:generator}).
Each doctor has one of eight specialties, which sets the distribution of
claim amounts, inpatient probability and procedure counts. Patients have
heterogeneous utilisation (gamma-distributed claim rates with mean three
claims per two years) and one to three regular doctors in their region;
85\% of legitimate claims go to a regular doctor and the rest to a
popularity-weighted random doctor. Claim amounts are log-normal given the
specialty, procedure count and inpatient status.

\paragraph{Isolated fraud.} Three per cent of claims are upcoded claims filed
through a patient's own regular doctor. Their log-amount, procedure count,
inpatient probability and procedure-code level are shifted by two standard
deviations; their structure is that of an ordinary claim.

\paragraph{Provider rings.} A ring consists of four colluding doctors and
twelve recruited patients, drawn preferentially from high utilisers. During
its active period the ring files 16 claims per month between linked
doctor--patient pairs, clustered around monthly burst days. Two parameters
are varied independently:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{feature shift} $\delta\in\{0, 1, 2\}$ applied to ring
  claims, in standard deviations of log-amount (with matching shifts in
  procedures, inpatient probability and coding level). At $\delta=0$ a ring
  claim is drawn from exactly the distribution of a legitimate claim of the
  same doctor's specialty;
\item the \emph{structural density} $s\in\{0.1, 0.5, 0.9\}$, the probability
  that a ring patient is linked to each ring doctor. Ring claim volume is
  fixed, so $s$ changes only how the claims are spread over the
  doctor--patient block (realised block densities $\approx 0.37$, $0.62$ and
  $0.79$).
\end{itemize}
Ring patients keep a single regular doctor, so the number of distinct
doctors they see is governed by $s$. Their elevated claim volume, a degree
signal visible to every model, is the same in all regimes.

\paragraph{Protocols.} Under the \emph{emergent} protocol (default), four new
rings start in each period and rings in different periods are formed by
disjoint doctors and patients, so every test ring is unseen during training.
Under the \emph{persistent} protocol the same four rings are active in all
periods, which reproduces the setting of most prior synthetic evaluations
and lets a model succeed by recognising known members. Both protocols have
identical claim and ring-claim volumes per period. Ground truth (fraud type
and ring identifier) is stored separately from the claim records and never
enters feature construction.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
Periods 1--2 are used for training, period 3 for validation (early stopping,
threshold selection) and period 4 for testing. Each period is analysed on its
own, as in a retrospective audit: a claim's aggregate and graph features, and
the graph a GNN operates on, are built only from claims of the same period.
This keeps every split's features on the same time scale and prevents
information from later periods reaching earlier ones; it is verified by
automated tests (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Tabular features}\label{sec:features}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C} (claim): log-amount, procedures, length of stay, inpatient flag,
  coding level, month (cyclical), patient age, sex and chronic-condition count,
  doctor experience and specialty, hospital size.
\item \textbf{A} (aggregates within the period, no labels): the patient's claim
  count, distinct doctors and mean/maximum log-amount; the doctor's claim
  count, distinct patients and mean log-amount; hospital claim count;
  patient--doctor pair count; and the claim's log-amount relative to the
  patient, doctor and specialty means.
\item \textbf{G} (graph features on the period's patient--doctor bipartite graph,
  no labels): the doctor's maximum shared-patient Jaccard similarity with
  another doctor and number of doctors sharing at least two patients, the
  mean degree of the doctor's patients, the patient's mean doctor overlap and
  share of doctor pairs that co-treat other patients, and the triadic closure
  of the claim (how many of the patient's other doctors share patients with
  the claim's doctor), raw and degree-normalised.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Heterogeneous graph}\label{sec:graph}
Each period's graph has patient, doctor, hospital and claim nodes and the
relations \emph{files} (patient$\to$claim), \emph{billed\_by}
(claim$\to$doctor), \emph{affiliated} (doctor$\to$hospital) and \emph{visits}
(patient$\to$doctor), each with a reverse relation so that every node type
receives messages. Claim nodes carry claim-intrinsic attributes; entity nodes
carry their static attributes and $\log(1+\text{degree})$ for each incident
relation, a label-free structural encoding included because attention- and
mean-based aggregators cannot otherwise count neighbours~\cite{xu2019gin}.
No engineered aggregates or labels are used as node features (Fig.~\ref{fig:schema}). Standardisation
statistics are fitted on training data.

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:models}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tabular}: logistic regression (LR) and random forest (RF) on C+A;
  histogram gradient boosting (GB)~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} on C, C+A and C+A+G.
\item \textbf{MLP-C}: a multilayer perceptron on C, the no-graph control for the GNNs.
\item \textbf{GNNs}: heterogeneous GraphSAGE~\cite{hamilton2017sage} (one convolution
  per relation, mean or sum aggregation), RGCN~\cite{schlichtkrull2018}, HAN~\cite{wang2019han}
  (claim nodes; meta-paths claim--patient--claim and claim--doctor--claim, with
  patient, doctor and hospital attributes appended to claim inputs),
  Simple-HGN~\cite{lv2021} and HGT~\cite{hu2020hgt}. All share the same
  per-type input projection, residual connections with layer normalisation,
  and two-layer classification head.
\item \textbf{Hybrids}: SAGE+AG and HGT+AG, whose claim nodes also receive the
  A and G features, testing whether message passing adds information beyond
  the engineered features. Hybrids reuse the tuned configuration of their base
  architecture and receive no separate tuning budget.
\item \textbf{Overlap score}: the degree-normalised triadic closure of the claim,
  used directly as an unsupervised score.
\end{itemize}
Tabular models use scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011} and graph models PyTorch
Geometric~\cite{fey2019}. Neural models are trained full-batch with AdamW,
class-weighted binary cross-entropy and early stopping on validation average
precision (patience 20, at most 200 epochs).

\paragraph{Hyperparameters.} Every method receives the same budget of up to
ten configurations (the first being a default), selected by mean validation
average precision on two development worlds (generator seeds 901 and 902,
$\delta=0.5$, $s=0.5$) that are never used for evaluation
(Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}, Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}). The development regime ($\delta=0.5$) is
deliberately not one of the evaluation regimes.

\subsection{Two-stage routing}\label{sec:routing}
A common deployment proposal is to score every claim with a cheap tabular
model and pass only high-risk claims to a GNN. We evaluate this with GB-CAG
as the first stage and HGT or SAGE as the second. A claim is escalated if its
GB-CAG score is at least the $(1-e)$ quantile of the GB-CAG scores in the
validation period. The escalation fraction $e\in\{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5,
1\}$ is chosen by validation AP, so test claims never influence it. Escalated
claims rank above all other claims and are ordered either by the GNN score
alone (\emph{re-rank}: GB-CAG acts as a filter) or by the mean of the two
models' validation percentiles (\emph{fused}); non-escalated claims keep
their GB-CAG order. With $e=1$, re-ranking reduces to the GNN alone and
fusion to a rank-average ensemble of the two models, so both single models
and the ensemble are among the candidates. A claim's routed score depends
only on its own two scores and on validation statistics. The stages are the
models of the main experiment, retrained with the same seeds; their test
predictions reproduce the stored ones exactly for the GNNs and to within
$3\times10^{-8}$ for GB-CAG (float32 storage).

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}\label{sec:stats}
The primary metric is average precision (AP, the area under the
precision--recall curve), appropriate under class imbalance~\cite{saito2015pr}.
\emph{Ring AP} scores ring claims against legitimate claims (isolated fraud
excluded); \emph{isolated AP} is defined analogously. We also report F1 at
the threshold that maximises validation F1, ROC-AUC and recall when the top
5\% of claims are investigated. For routing we add precision among the top
5\% of claims (P@5\%) and, as a ring-level measure, the share of test rings
with at least one claim in that budget. Each of the nine regimes is replicated with
six independently generated worlds (generator seeds 1--6), 54 worlds in total. Methods are compared within a
regime with paired $t$-tests across worlds; $p$-values are Holm-adjusted
within each pre-specified family of comparisons~\cite{holm1979}, and
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests are reported as a robustness check~\cite{demsar2006}.
Intervals are 95\% $t$-intervals over worlds.

\subsection{Use of AI tools}\label{sec:aitools}
An AI coding assistant (Claude, Anthropic) was used to help write and review
the analysis code, run and document the experiments, and draft and edit parts
of the text. All reported numbers are produced by the released code
(Section~\ref{sec:repro}). The authors reviewed all code, analyses and text
and take full responsibility for the content.


\section{Real-World Data}\label{sec:realdata}

\subsection{Data}
We use the public ``Healthcare Provider Fraud Detection Analysis''
dataset~\cite{kaggle2019} (CC0 public domain), which links Medicare-style
inpatient and outpatient claims to beneficiaries and labels each provider as
potentially fraudulent or not. Only the labelled training files are used:
558{,}211 claims (40{,}474 inpatient, 517{,}737 outpatient) dated between
November~2008 and December~2009, filed by 5{,}410 providers (506, or 9.4\%,
labelled fraudulent) for 138{,}556 beneficiaries, with 100{,}737 distinct
attending, operating or other physicians. The data documentation does not
describe how the labels were obtained, so we treat them as given and
discuss this limitation in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Because labels
exist only per provider, the prediction unit is the provider; claim-level
ring detection cannot be evaluated on these data.

\subsection{Features and graph}
\textbf{P} (23 provider aggregates): claim volume and inpatient share,
beneficiaries and claims per beneficiary, distinct attending and operating
physicians, reimbursement totals and log-amount statistics, deductibles,
claim duration and length of stay, diagnosis and procedure counts, share of
claims with an operating physician, and the age, chronic-condition burden,
mortality, renal-disease share, annual reimbursements and geographic spread
of the provider's beneficiaries. \textbf{G} (8 label-free graph features):
for both shared beneficiaries and shared physicians, the number of
co-providers, the maximum Jaccard overlap with another provider, the share
of the provider's beneficiaries (physicians) seen by other providers, and
their mean number of providers. \textbf{L} (4 label-neighbourhood
features): the share of co-providers that are known fraudulent
\emph{training} providers, and an indicator of having any labelled
co-provider, through beneficiaries and through physicians. L uses only
training labels and never a provider's own label.

The heterogeneous graph has provider, beneficiary and physician nodes, with
relations \emph{serves} (provider--beneficiary, 363{,}300 edges) and
\emph{employs} (provider--physician) plus reverse relations. Physicians
working for a single provider carry no cross-provider information and are
summarised in P rather than kept as nodes, leaving 7{,}685 shared physicians
(16{,}287 edges). Provider nodes carry P (or P+G for the hybrids),
beneficiary nodes 22 demographic and clinical attributes, and physician nodes
their claim and provider counts. HAN uses the provider-only meta-paths
provider--beneficiary--provider and provider--physician--provider.

\subsection{Protocol}
All providers' claims are observed and define the graph; labels of
validation and test providers are hidden. This matches an audit in which
claims are available for every provider but only some providers have been
investigated. Providers are assigned to five stratified folds; in rotation
$k$, fold $k$ is the test set, fold $k+1$ the validation set and the
remaining three folds the training set, so every provider is tested exactly
once. Hyperparameters are selected once, with the same budget of six
configurations per method, on a separate stratified 60/20/20 split
(seed~1000), and then fixed for the five rotations. Graph models are
trained full-batch for at most 100 epochs with early stopping (patience 10)
on validation AP. Metrics are AP, ROC-AUC, F1 at the validation-selected
threshold, and recall among the top 10\% of providers by score (the label
prevalence is 9.4\%). Methods are compared with paired $t$-tests over the
five rotations (Holm-adjusted); with five rotations these tests have
limited power and are interpreted together with effect sizes.

\subsection{Claim features alone do not reveal rings}
When ring claims are drawn like legitimate claims ($\delta=0$), models that
see only the claim's own attributes perform at chance (GB-C 0.084--0.087,
MLP-C 0.087--0.095). As $\delta$ grows their ring AP rises to about 0.33
($\delta=1$) and 0.81 ($\delta=2$). This confirms that the benchmark
separates the two sources of evidence as intended.

\subsection{Most ring evidence is captured by entity aggregates}
Adding per-period aggregates of the claim's patient, doctor and hospital
(GB-CA) raises ring AP at $\delta=0$ to 0.889--0.925. Ring patients file
more claims and see more doctors than typical patients, and ring doctors
file bursts of claims; these degree and volume signals are present in every
regime by design and are captured by simple counts. The remaining gap is
where coordination structure matters.

\subsection{Coordination structure helps only when the ring block is dense}
Engineered graph features add little at low density but substantially at
moderate and high density: at $\delta=0$, GB-CAG improves on GB-CA by
$+0.005$ ($s=0.1$, n.s.), $+0.078$ ($s=0.5$) and $+0.101$ ($s=0.9$; Holm
$p=0.003$). The unsupervised overlap score tells the same story: it is weak
at $s=0.1$ (0.254) but reaches 0.937 and 0.985 at $s=0.5$ and $0.9$ without
any training. GNNs show the same pattern: SAGE improves on GB-CA by
$+0.087$ at $\delta=0$, $s=0.9$ (Holm $p=0.003$). Pooled over all worlds,
every graph-aware method except HAN outperforms GB-CA (GB-CAG $+0.031$, SAGE
$+0.031$, RGCN $+0.026$, Simple-HGN $+0.024$, HGT $+0.021$, hybrids $+0.039$;
all Holm $p<0.001$; HAN $+0.007$, n.s.). When ring claims are strongly anomalous
($\delta=2$), all methods that see claim and aggregate features exceed 0.98
and the differences become negligible.

\subsection{GNNs do not outperform gradient boosting with graph features}
The central comparison is between message passing and the same structural
information supplied as engineered features. Pooled over the 54 worlds,
heterogeneous GraphSAGE matches GB-CAG ($\Delta=-0.0001$, 95\% CI
$[-0.005, 0.005]$), RGCN is not significantly different ($-0.005$), and
Simple-HGN ($-0.008$, Holm $p=0.024$), HGT ($-0.011$, $[-0.020,-0.002]$,
Holm $p=0.060$) and HAN ($-0.024$, Holm $p<0.001$) are lower. Within single
regimes, where each test has only six worlds, no GNN is significantly
better than GB-CAG in any regime; SAGE, HAN and Simple-HGN are significantly
worse at $\delta=0$, $s=0.5$, and SAGE and Simple-HGN at $\delta=0$, $s=0.9$
(Holm-adjusted within each nine-regime family).

The hybrids, which add message passing on top of the engineered features,
gain a small but consistent amount over GB-CAG (SAGE+AG $+0.008$
$[0.003, 0.013]$, Holm $p=0.013$; HGT+AG $+0.008$ $[0.003, 0.013]$, Holm
$p=0.019$), largest in the sparse-structure, no-feature-signal regime
($\delta=0$, $s=0.1$: SAGE+AG $+0.043$, unadjusted $p=0.011$, Holm $p=0.61$).
Message passing therefore carries a little information beyond our
engineered features, but in this benchmark it does not replace them.

\subsection{Routing claims to a GNN adds little beyond combining scores}\label{sec:routingres}
Table~\ref{tab:cascade} and Fig.~\ref{fig:cascade} evaluate two-stage
routing (Section~\ref{sec:routing}) on the same test claims as the single
models. In the design usually proposed, GB-CAG filters and the GNN decides
(re-rank). Validation mostly selected small escalation fractions (5\% in 30
of 54 worlds for HGT), and the routed system matched GB-CAG (GB-CAG$\to$HGT
$\Delta\text{AP}=-0.001$, 95\% CI $[-0.004, 0.002]$; GB-CAG$\to$SAGE
$+0.001$). Escalating more claims to HGT lowered both ring AP (from 0.980 at
5\% to 0.968 when every claim is escalated) and, more steeply, isolated AP
(from 0.626 to 0.508; Fig.~\ref{fig:cascade}b--d), because HGT ranks both
less well than GB-CAG. Ordering escalated claims by the mean of the two models' percentiles
did better: GB-CAG$\to$HGT gained $+0.003$ (Holm $p=0.18$) and
GB-CAG$\to$SAGE $+0.006$ $[0.004, 0.009]$ (Holm $p<0.001$), on ring ($+0.006$)
and isolated fraud ($+0.023$) alike. Validation, however, chose to fuse
\emph{every} claim in 22 (HGT) and 30 (SAGE) of the 54 worlds, and on average
55\% and 70\% of test claims were escalated: the gain comes from combining two
different rankings, not from routing a subset of claims. The feature-level
hybrids, which give the GNN the engineered features, improved ring AP
($+0.008$, Holm $p\le0.024$) but lost 0.08--0.11 isolated AP and so matched
GB-CAG overall. At the ring level every method was equivalent: in all 54
worlds each test ring had at least one claim among the top 5\% of claims, and
precision in that budget was 0.989--0.998 for every method.

\subsection{Cost}
Median training time per world (Intel Core i7-1068NG7 laptop CPU, two
threads per job with several jobs in parallel, so times are indicative) was
3~s for GB-CAG, 39~s for SAGE, 56~s for RGCN, 86~s for HAN, 126~s for HGT and
144~s for Simple-HGN; scoring a test period took at most 0.25~s for any
method. A separate single-thread benchmark on an otherwise idle machine (four
worlds at $\delta=0$) measured scoring directly. For one audit period (about
4{,}900 claims and 11{,}200 nodes), building the aggregate and graph features
took 0.05~s and the heterogeneous graph 0.01~s, and scoring every claim took
0.05~s with GB-CAG and 0.12~s with HGT. Scoring a single claim, as an online
system would, took a median of 2.1~ms (99th percentile 3.1~ms, mostly
library-call overhead) with GB-CAG on a precomputed feature row, and 10.7~ms
(14.7~ms) with HGT on the claim's exact three-hop computation subgraph (about
126 nodes, 1\% of the period graph; extraction 2.8~ms, forward pass 7.7~ms),
which reproduces the full-graph score to within $1.2\times10^{-7}$. Routing
would therefore cut the online GNN cost in proportion to the share of claims
escalated. In batch scoring the escalated claims' subgraphs overlap:
escalating 10\% of the claims already touched 55\% of the period graph, and
scoring that union took 0.08~s against 0.12~s for the whole period. None of
these figures includes keeping aggregates, graph features and degree counts
up to date as claims arrive, which an online system would need and our code
does not implement.


\section{Mechanism and Memorisation}\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{What isolated and ring fraud look like}
Fig.~\ref{fig:ringgraph} shows the test period of one world (seed 1,
$\delta=0$) with the outcomes of GB-CAG and HGT at their validation-selected
thresholds. An isolated fraud claim sits on an ordinary patient--doctor
edge, so nothing in the graph distinguishes it and only its own attributes
can. A ring at $s=0.1$ links each recruited patient to one or two of the four
ring doctors, a pattern that resembles ordinary shared care, whereas at
$s=0.9$ the same members form an almost complete block. The two worlds share
the recruited ring members and every legitimate and isolated-fraud claim, and
differ only in which ring links exist. In this world GB-CAG flagged 81 and 91 of the ring's 96 claims at
$s=0.1$ and $s=0.9$, and HGT 62 and 94: the graph model gains most where the
block is dense, as in the averages of Table~\ref{tab:regime_ringap}. The
figure illustrates one world only; the quantitative statements rest on all
54.

\subsection{What the models need}
Tables~\ref{tab:ablation} and~\ref{tab:hgt_arch} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}
report ablations in the regime where structure matters most ($\delta=0$,
$s=0.9$).
Degree-preserving rewiring---permuting which doctor bills each claim within a
period, which keeps every patient's and doctor's claim volume but destroys
the ring's co-occurrence block---reduces every graph-aware method to the same
level (0.757--0.770), close to gradient boosting without graph features after
rewiring (0.748). The GNNs therefore do exploit coordination structure, and
once it is removed nothing distinguishes them from a model that sees volume
alone.

Depth matters: with a single message-passing layer, ring AP falls by 0.135
(SAGE) and 0.161 (HGT); two and three layers perform alike. Removing the
direct patient--doctor relation costs little ($-0.016$ and $-0.047$, not
significant after correction), because two-hop paths through claim nodes
carry the same information. The largest effect is specific to HGT: without
degree encodings its ring AP drops from 0.949 to 0.569 ($-0.379$, Holm
$p=0.003$), whereas sum-aggregating SAGE changes little ($-0.029$, n.s.).
Softmax attention normalises over neighbours and so cannot count them;
supplying counts as input features, or aggregating by summation, restores
the signal~\cite{xu2019gin}.

\subsection{Which parts of HGT matter}\label{sec:hgtarch}
Table~\ref{tab:hgt_arch} changes one factor of the tuned HGT at a time in
the same regime. Depth beyond two layers and the number of attention heads
made no significant difference (four layers $+0.002$; one, two and eight
heads $-0.028$ to $+0.013$; all Holm $p>0.5$), so there is no sign of
over-smoothing up to four layers on these graphs. Among the relations,
removing the patient--claim edges cost most ($-0.219$, Holm $p=0.005$);
removing the claim--doctor edges cost $0.113$ on rings (Holm $p=0.085$) and
$0.147$ on isolated fraud ($p<0.001$), while hospital nodes and the direct
patient--doctor relation contributed nothing measurable. The node inputs
separate the two tasks cleanly. With claim attributes set to zero, ring AP
was unchanged ($-0.012$) and isolated AP fell to chance (0.036); an HGT that
saw only the degree encodings and the graph reached a ring AP of
$0.982\pm0.018$, as high as the full model, and detected no isolated fraud.
HGT's ring detection is therefore structural---counts of who bills whom,
propagated along patient--claim--doctor paths---and its claim attributes
serve isolated fraud only. This is the information that the engineered
shared-patient features summarise directly. HGT also varied between worlds
(standard deviations up to 0.11), so with six worlds differences smaller than
about 0.05 cannot be resolved.

\subsection{Which inputs the trained models use}\label{sec:reliance}
To see which inputs the trained models use, without retraining, we permuted
one input group at a time across the test period, five times each, and
recorded the drop in ring AP: for GB-CAG the C, A and G features and each G
feature separately, and for the GNNs the claim attributes, static entity
attributes and degree encodings across nodes of each type. At $\delta=0$,
GB-CAG relied on aggregates when rings were sparse (A: $-0.80$ at $s=0.1$;
G: $-0.13$) and switched to the graph features as the block became dense (G:
$-0.81$ and $-0.88$ at $s=0.5$ and $0.9$; A: below $0.06$), above all the
normalised triadic closure of the claim ($-0.47$ on its own at $s=0.9$).
SAGE and HGT relied almost entirely on degree encodings ($-0.76$ to $-0.89$
at every density) and hardly at all on claim or entity attributes (below
$0.05$). The two kinds of score are not on a common scale: GB-CAG's measure
reliance on engineered summaries of the graph, whereas a GNN's degree
encodings are raw counts whose relational meaning is extracted by message
passing, and permutation understates groups whose information is duplicated
elsewhere. Read together with the ablations, they are consistent with both
model families detecting dense rings from the same evidence: how many
counterparties a claim's patient and doctor have, and how many of them they
share. (Full results, including isolated fraud and test-time rewiring, are in
the repository.)

\subsection{Two evaluation pitfalls, quantified}
The mechanism ablations (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) also quantify two defects that are easy to introduce. Adding
the generator's ring identifier to the feature set---a column that is
non-zero only for ring claims---raises gradient boosting from 0.889 to a
perfect 1.000. Building the heterogeneous graph with one-directional
relations only (patient$\to$claim$\to$doctor$\to$hospital) leaves claim nodes
reachable only from patients, so doctor context never reaches them; HGT then
loses 0.177 AP. Both defects produce plausible-looking numbers and neither is
caught by aggregate metrics alone; the tests listed in
Section~\ref{sec:repro} detect both.

\subsection{Recognising known members is not detecting rings}
Table~\ref{tab:protocol} and Fig.~\ref{fig:protocolres} compare new rings (emergent protocol) with rings
whose members recur from training (persistent protocol). The MLP control,
which sees only the claim and the static attributes of its patient, doctor
and hospital, rises from chance to 0.692 ($s=0.1$) and 0.591 ($s=0.9$) when
rings recur: it identifies the members from their attributes. HGT gains
0.056 at $s=0.1$ (95\% CI $[0.029, 0.084]$), and gradient boosting with
aggregates gains 0.035--0.063, while the unsupervised overlap score, which
cannot memorise, is unchanged. Evaluations in which test rings share members
with training rings therefore overstate how well a model detects
coordination, by an amount that differs between methods and can reorder
them.

\section{Results on Real Data}\label{sec:realresults}

Table~\ref{tab:kaggle} and Fig.~\ref{fig:kaggle} report provider-level
results over the five rotations (1{,}082 test providers each, about 101
fraudulent). Gradient boosting on provider aggregates (GB-P) has the highest
AP ($0.704\pm0.045$). Adding label-free graph features (GB-PG, $-0.009$,
95\% CI $[-0.029, 0.011]$) or graph and label-neighbourhood features

AP than GB-P, by $-0.029$ (HGT) to $-0.083$ (RGCN); with five rotations most
of these differences are not significant after Holm adjustment over all 24
comparisons, the exception being SAGE+G ($-0.051$, Holm $p=0.021$). The
hybrids, which give the GNN the provider and graph features, are no better
than the plain GNNs. ROC-AUC lies between 0.927 and 0.942 for all methods and
recall among the top 10\% of providers between 0.59 and 0.64, so the methods
rank providers similarly; the AP differences come from the top of the
ranking. Under the same parallel load as before, median training time per
rotation was 2--15 minutes for the GNNs (range 1.4--24 minutes) against 7~s
for GB-P. Because the tuning split shares providers with the test folds, we
re-selected the configuration of every tabular and MLP model inside each
rotation, using only that rotation's validation fold. AP changed by at most
0.010 (GB-P $0.706$ versus $0.704$; no paired difference significant), and
GB-P remained the best method, so the overlap does not explain the ranking.

resemble the low-density regime, where structure added nothing beyond

without identifying rings, so the absence of a graph benefit here does not
show that rings are absent from Medicare data; it shows that this public
labelling contains no exploitable relational signal for the models tested.


\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{When does relational structure help?}
Three conditions emerge from the controlled study. First, coordination must
leave a structural trace beyond volume: at low block density, rings were
detected mainly through the elevated utilisation of their members, which
per-entity counts capture. Second, the trace must be dense enough to
distinguish a ring from ordinary shared care: graph information added
0.04--0.10 AP at moderate and high density (most when ring claims looked
legitimate) but nothing measurable at low density. Third, the claims must not already be anomalous on their own: when
ring claims were strongly upcoded ($\delta=2$), every model with claim and
aggregate features exceeded 0.98 AP and structure became redundant.

\subsection{Why GNNs did not beat engineered graph features}
When structure did help, gradient boosting with a handful of shared-patient
statistics captured it as well as or better than the best GNN. The mechanism
analyses suggest why. The useful signal here is largely
\emph{counting}---how many of a patient's doctors also share other
patients---and attention- or mean-based aggregators cannot count: HGT lost
most of its ring detection without degree encodings, whereas sum-aggregating
GraphSAGE barely changed, and an HGT given only degree encodings and the
graph detected rings as well as the full model (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). This mirrors the
expressiveness argument for sum aggregation~\cite{xu2019gin}. GNNs also
dilute claim-level anomalies through neighbourhood averaging, which made
them markedly worse on isolated fraud, and they readily memorise the
attributes of training-ring members, which inflates performance when rings
recur but does not transfer to new rings. Hybrids that combine message
passing with engineered features recovered a small, consistent gain over
gradient boosting on ring fraud, indicating that the GNN extracted some
structure our features miss, at 9--40 times the training cost.

These findings are consistent with evidence from other domains that tree
ensembles with neighbourhood features are strong graph anomaly
detectors~\cite{weber2019elliptic,tang2023gadbench}, and extend it to
heterogeneous patient--provider claim graphs with controlled typologies.

\subsection{Why a small fixed benchmark can point the other way}\label{sec:legacy}
To show how a small, fixed benchmark can mislead, we re-analysed one: a
synthetic dataset of 2{,}100 claims with five injected rings, produced by a
simpler generator that is included in our repository, evaluated with its own
date-sorted 60/20/20 split (Table~\ref{tab:legacy}). Such a dataset appears
to test ring detection, but this one cannot tell whether relational
structure helps, for three reasons. First, its ring claims were drawn from the
same inflated distribution as its isolated fraud, so they were anomalous on
their own: claim amount alone separates ring from legitimate claims with
ROC-AUC 0.94, more than in our most anomalous regime ($\delta=2$, 0.90), and
gradient boosting on three raw claim attributes ranks all 21 test ring claims
above every legitimate claim (ring AP 1.000). Second, the same five rings
were active for all 24 months, so every test ring claim was billed by a
doctor with labelled ring claims in training, which is the memorisation
setting of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. Third, the test split held 21 ring
claims (3--6 per ring): one claim moves ring recall by 4.8 points, even a
perfect recall has an exact 95\% interval of $[0.84, 1.00]$, so a recall gap
of a few claims between two models is within noise, and a single dataset says
nothing about variation between datasets. None of these properties is
peculiar to that dataset. A small, fixed benchmark whose injected rings
differ from legitimate claims in their features, recur across the split and
contribute a few dozen test claims cannot separate relational learning from
claim-level anomaly detection or from recognising known members. Controlling
the feature-level signal, testing on new rings and replicating worlds
removes all three confounds. Because the generator, features, evaluation unit
and tuning differ, numbers from the two set-ups are not directly comparable.

\subsection{Real data}
On the public provider-labelled data, gradient boosting on provider
aggregates was the strongest method, and neither engineered graph features,
label-neighbourhood features nor any GNN improved on it. The labels are not
assortative through shared beneficiaries, and fraudulent providers differ
mainly in volume, so the data sit in the regime where our benchmark also
found no benefit from structure. This is consistent with the controlled
results but is not an independent confirmation of the dense-ring regime:
validating that regime requires real data with labelled collusion, which are
not publicly available.

\subsection{Practical recommendations}
\begin{itemize}
\item Start with gradient boosting on claim features, per-entity aggregates
  and a small set of shared-patient graph features; on our benchmark this was
  never significantly worse than any pure GNN, within 0.01 AP of the hybrids
  on average, and far cheaper.
\item Evaluate on rings (or providers) that do not appear in training, and
  compare against a no-graph control with identical inputs; otherwise
  memorisation of known members can masquerade as relational learning.
\item If a GNN is used, include reverse relations, verify that information
  reaches the classified nodes, and give attention-based architectures
  structural (degree) encodings or prefer sum aggregation.
\item Keep isolated-fraud detection on a claim-level model; message passing
  degraded it consistently.
\item Do not expect a GNN second stage to pay for itself. Letting a GNN
  re-rank the claims that gradient boosting flags gained nothing here;
  averaging the two models' rankings gained at most 0.006 AP, and in an audit
  setting, where a whole period is scored at once, routing saves little
  computation (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
The controlled findings hold for our generator. Its distributions are
simple, labels are noise-free and immediate, rings are static during their
active period and of a single size, and ring prevalence (about 8\% of
test-period claims) is far above realistic levels so that each world has
enough positives for stable estimates. Ring members also carry a volume
signal in every regime, so our regimes vary the \emph{additional} structural
evidence rather than all structural evidence. We tuned each method once, on
development worlds from a single regime ($\delta=0.5$, $s=0.5$); per-regime
tuning could change small differences. Six worlds per regime give limited
power within a regime, which is why we emphasise pooled comparisons. Graph
models were trained full-batch on graphs of about 11{,}000 nodes per period;
larger graphs would need sampling-based training~\cite{hamilton2017sage}.
The architecture and reliance analyses were run in one regime ($\delta=0$,
$s=0.9$ for the ablations) with one model seed per world, and permutation
importance can understate a feature group whose information is duplicated
in another; these analyses describe what the trained models use, not causal
effects of the generator. Routing was evaluated with one first stage, two
GNNs and a grid of six escalation fractions; the GNNs' validation scores come
from the period used for early stopping, which slightly favours escalation.
Cost was measured on a single laptop CPU thread and excludes the upkeep of
aggregates, graph features and degree counts as claims arrive, data
retrieval and concurrency, none of which exist in our code.
On the real data, labels are per provider and of undocumented provenance,
and the observation window is a single year. Hyperparameters were selected
on a split whose validation providers overlap the evaluation test folds;
re-selecting the tabular and MLP configurations inside each rotation
(Section~\ref{sec:realresults}) removes this overlap for those models, but
the GNNs keep the shared selection, which if anything favours them.
Finally, the benchmark does not model adaptive fraudsters, temporal ring
dynamics, which temporal graph models target~\cite{rossi2020tgn}, or
realistic coding semantics such as those of synthetic health
records~\cite{walonoski2018synthea}.


\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}
All results are produced by one released package from raw inputs. Every run
writes a record containing the git commit, a hash of its configuration, the
hashes of the generated claim and ground-truth tables, the library versions
and the per-claim (or per-provider) test predictions; the tables and figures
of this paper are generated from these records by a single script. Automated
tests, run in continuous integration, check that (i) no ground-truth column
reaches any feature matrix and features are invariant to permuting the labels;
(ii) a period's features and graph are unchanged when claims of other periods
are perturbed; (iii) emergent rings in different splits share no doctor or
patient; (iv) at $\delta=0$ ring claims are distributed like legitimate
claims; (v) features, graphs and labels stay aligned by claim identifier; and
(vi) for every GNN, perturbing doctor features changes claim logits, so
relational information actually reaches the classified nodes; (vii) a routed
score depends only on the claim's own two scores and validation statistics;
(viii) rewiring the test period leaves the features of other periods
unchanged; and (ix) scoring a claim on its $L$-hop computation subgraph
reproduces its full-graph score. The original runs were made from a working
tree that had not yet been committed, so their records do not pin the code
version; retraining GB-CAG, SAGE and HGT with the released code on all 54
worlds reproduced their stored test predictions (Section~\ref{sec:routing}),
which ties the reported numbers to the released code. Records now also store a
hash of the research sources at process start. A checklist
mapping this study to the REFORMS recommendations~\cite{kapoor2024reforms}
and a datasheet for the generator~\cite{gebru2021datasheets} are included in
the repository.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We asked when relational structure helps healthcare fraud detection and
answered it with a benchmark that varies the feature-level and structural
signal of provider rings independently, tests on rings never seen in
training, and checks the conclusions on public real data. Structure helped
only when rings formed dense doctor--patient blocks, and in that regime a
gradient-boosting model with a handful of engineered shared-patient features
matched or outperformed five heterogeneous GNNs at a fraction of the cost;
adding message passing on top of those features, or routing high-risk claims
to a GNN, gained little. GNNs
generalised to new rings only with reverse relations, sufficient depth and
counting ability, degraded isolated-fraud detection, and could memorise known
ring members in ways that inflate conventional evaluations. A small fixed
benchmark whose rings are anomalous in their features, recur across the split
and contribute few test claims can suggest the opposite conclusion. On
provider-labelled real data no graph-based method improved on tabular
aggregates. We recommend
that graph models for claims fraud be justified against a graph-augmented
tabular baseline and a no-graph control, on entities unseen in training. The
benchmark, code and tests are released to support such comparisons; the most
valuable next step is real data with labelled collusive schemes.
